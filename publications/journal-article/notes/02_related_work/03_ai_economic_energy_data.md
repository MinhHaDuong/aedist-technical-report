## Relevance

This note supports paragraph 3 of §2. The paragraph must position our work against the existing landscape of energy infrastructure databases and closely related projects, making two specific claims: (1) the authoritative databases from which energy system models draw their power-plant inventories are hand-curated by expert teams, not generated by AI; and (2) the closely related open-energy-system projects — powerplantmatching, PyPSA-Earth, PyPSA-VN — consume these hand-curated databases without automating their production. The paragraph then sets up the gap statement (paragraph 4): to our knowledge, no prior work benchmarks AI-assisted production of such tables against an expert-compiled gold reference.

The intellectual stakes are concrete. A referee might object that hand-curated databases are well-known and that the paragraph is obvious. A second referee might note that PyPSA-VN uses Vietnamese thermal plant data for the same country as our pilot task, suggesting redundancy with our work. The paragraph must cite PyPSA-VN explicitly and distinguish it: PyPSA-VN builds on manually compiled plant data; we benchmark AI-assisted production of exactly that kind of data.

The five mandatory citations (all ticket-specified) span the relevant literature: two hand-curated databases we use as gold references (GEM, WRI GPPD), one algorithmic-merging tool that combines hand-curated databases (powerplantmatching), one global open model that consumes these databases without automating their production (PyPSA-Earth), and one closely-related project in the same country (PyPSA-VN). The citation mix satisfies the seminal + established + recent/domain-specific requirement.


## History of science context

**Pre-2010: Proprietary plant databases and national registries.** Power plant inventories were historically proprietary. S&P Global (formerly Platts) World Electric Power Plants (WEPP) database and IEA plant-level data were available only to subscribers. National grid operators maintained their own registries — EIA Form 860 in the United States, the ENTSOE Transparency Platform in Europe — but cross-country comparisons required licensed access or laborious manual assembly. Researchers working on energy system models outside the OECD core had very limited options.

**2010–2015: Open data movement; first global compilations.** The open data movement reached the energy sector. Enipedia (TU Delft, ~2010), CARMA (CO2 emissions from power plants, 2007–2012), and the Global Energy Observatory attempted open power plant inventories at varying quality levels. All relied on volunteer contributions, web scraping, and manual verification; none had a systematic methodology for handling conflicting data across sources. The inconsistency of these early resources motivated later systematic efforts.

**2015–2019: Expert-curated databases with open methodology.** Two developments reshaped the landscape. First, the World Resources Institute released the Global Power Plant Database (GPPD) in 2018 — a methodically assembled dataset of ~30,000 plants across 164 countries, built by a team of researchers using primary government sources, satellite imagery cross-checking, and a documented cleaning process. Second, the Global Energy Monitor (formerly CoalSwarm) expanded from tracking coal to tracking gas, oil, and eventually all power generation, using a wiki-based model where volunteer researchers make edits that are validated by expert coordinators. Both are continuously updated and openly licensed.

**2018–2019: Algorithmic merging of hand-curated sources.** Recognizing that each database has coverage gaps and quality variations by country and fuel type, Gotzens et al. (2019) developed powerplantmatching: a Python tool that systematically merges five open databases (OPSD, ENTSOE, GEO, CARMA, WRI) using fuzzy name matching, capacity-based deduplication, and configurable inclusion rules. The tool introduces a reproducibility standard for European energy modelling: researchers can trace each plant record to its source database and see how conflicts were resolved. This is algorithmic merging of hand-curated inputs, not AI generation of new data.

**2020–2024: Open global models consuming the curated databases.** PyPSA-Earth (Parzen et al. 2023) scaled the PyPSA framework from Europe to any country in the world, demonstrating its approach on Africa. For conventional generator data, PyPSA-Earth builds on powerplantmatching, which in turn draws on WRI GPPD and GEM trackers. The pipeline is automated in the sense of scripted data retrieval and cleaning, but the underlying plant records in WRI and GEM were assembled by human expert teams. PyPSA-VN (Schlott et al. 2020) applied a similar philosophy to Vietnam specifically, assembling a thermal power plant inventory for the Vietnamese electricity system to enable energy planning analysis. The plant data in PyPSA-VN were manually compiled from Vietnamese government sources and published reports.

**The gap.** None of these projects benchmarks AI systems as agents that discover and tabulate power plant data from open sources. The human-curation step is assumed: GEM editors verify each plant against government documents; WRI researchers cross-check satellite imagery; PyPSA-VN authors compiled the Vietnamese plant list by reading government documents. Our benchmark asks whether a large language model, prompted appropriately, can replicate the output of that human curation process — and provides a gold reference (derived from GEM and WRI GPPD) to measure recall against.


## Cited works — detailed

### Gotzens et al. 2019 — powerplantmatching
*(existing key in refs.bib: LYGDCQCL)*

- **Reference.** Gotzens, F., Heinrichs, H., Hörsch, J., and Hofmann, F. (2019). Performing energy modelling exercises in a transparent way — The issue of data quality in power plant databases. *Energy Strategy Reviews*, 23, 1–12. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esr.2018.11.004
- **What the work did.** Develops powerplantmatching, an open-source Python library that downloads, cleans, and merges five open power plant databases for European conventional generators: Open Power System Data (OPSD), ENTSOE Transparency Platform, Global Energy Observatory (GEO), CARMA (CO2 emissions DB), and WRI GPPD. Uses fuzzy name matching and capacity-based deduplication to resolve conflicts. Analyses data quality along five dimensions: installed capacity by country, installed capacity by fuel type, geographic coordinates, commissioning year, and efficiency. Demonstrates that merged databases are more complete and consistent than individual sources.
- **Why this paragraph cites it.** powerplantmatching is the reference tool for combining open energy databases in European energy modelling, and it is an upstream dependency of PyPSA-Earth's conventional-generator pipeline. Citing it establishes the "algorithmic merging of hand-curated sources" paradigm clearly: the tool is sophisticated and reproducible, but it merges existing human-curated records — it does not generate new plant data. This is the methodological contrast we exploit: our task is to test whether AI can produce the kind of data that currently must be assembled by expert teams for input to tools like powerplantmatching.
- **Limitations or critiques.** Coverage is primarily European; the five merged databases have patchy coverage outside Europe, especially for Southeast Asia. The fuzzy-matching approach handles name variations and duplicate records but cannot recover plants that are simply absent from all five source databases. The tool is agnostic about source reliability — all five databases are treated as peers, though some are more carefully curated than others. The 2019 paper reflects database versions as of 2018; the tool is actively maintained but the paper itself is not updated.
- **Role in citation mix.** Seminal for the open energy database literature. The tool is the standard reference for transparent power plant data compilation in European energy system modelling.

### Global Energy Monitor (GEM) 2024 — Global Integrated Power Tracker
*(existing key in refs.bib: LCVI7NAT)*

- **Reference.** Global Energy Monitor. (2024). Global Integrated Power Tracker. URL: https://globalenergymonitor.org/projects/global-integrated-power-tracker/
- **What the work did.** GEM maintains a family of fuel-specific trackers — Global Coal Plant Tracker, Global Gas Plant Tracker, Global Oil Plant Tracker, and others — that are unified under the Global Integrated Power Tracker. Each tracker records plant-level data (capacity, status, commissioning date, ownership, geographic coordinates, fuel type, technology) for all power units globally above a minimum capacity threshold. Data collection uses a wiki-based workflow: GEM researchers gather primary data from government documents, national energy plans, company filings, and on-the-ground contacts, then validate against multiple independent sources before publication. Data are released semi-annually under Creative Commons licensing. The methodology page documents validation procedures, source hierarchy, and definitions for plant status categories (operating, under construction, pre-construction, shelved, cancelled, retired).
- **Why this paragraph cites it.** GEM trackers are one of the two primary gold references for our benchmark. The Vietnamese thermal power plant inventory against which we measure AI recall is derived in part from GEM data. Citing GEM establishes the nature of our gold reference: it is the product of expert human curation, not of automated generation. This is essential for the benchmark's validity claim — if the gold reference were itself AI-generated, the benchmark would be circular.
- **Limitations or critiques.** GEM's coverage of Southeast Asian plants, including Vietnam, is extensive but not exhaustive: minor plants below the capacity threshold, plants under local-language obscurity, and very recent additions may be missing or delayed relative to the publication cycle. The wiki-based model means data quality is heterogeneous across countries and depends on the availability of English-language sources and local expert contributors. The "Global Integrated Power Tracker" is a composite product; the individual fuel-specific trackers (Coal, Gas) are older and more granular.
- **Role in citation mix.** Established authoritative database. GEM is the primary community reference for global power plant tracking; it has no single publication DOI (it is a continuously updated database), which is noted in the bibliography.

### Byers et al. 2018 — Global Power Plant Database (WRI GPPD)
*(new key: Byers-Logan2018:wri-gppd)*

- **Reference.** Byers, L., Friedrich, J., Hennig, R., Kressig, A., Li, X., Malaguzzi Valeri, L., and McCormick, C. (2018). A Global Database of Power Plants. Technical note. Washington, DC: World Resources Institute. URL: https://www.wri.org/research/global-database-power-plants. Data repository: https://github.com/wri/global-power-plant-database
- **What the work did.** Assembles a systematic, open, globally-scoped power plant database covering approximately 30,000 plants across 164 countries as of the April 2018 release. Each record includes plant name, country, capacity (MW), primary fuel, latitude/longitude, commissioning year, ownership, and estimated annual generation. Data were collected from 60+ national government sources, utilities, regulatory filings, satellite imagery checks, and secondary databases. Cleaning procedures are documented: duplicate detection by name and geography, capacity normalization by fuel type, coordinate verification against satellite imagery. Released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0; maintained and updated via the WRI Open Data Portal and GitHub.
- **Why this paragraph cites it.** The WRI GPPD is the second primary gold reference for our benchmark and the most widely used open global power plant dataset in the energy modelling community. It is cited here to establish that the most complete, methodologically documented global plant database available is the product of expert human effort over multiple years — not of automated AI generation. PyPSA-Earth's conventional-generator pipeline draws on WRI GPPD (via powerplantmatching), and our gold standard for Vietnamese thermal plants is cross-referenced against GPPD. The WRI GPPD is the operationalization of "hand-curated authoritative database" in this paragraph.
- **Limitations or critiques.** The April 2018 version is the landmark reference, but the database has been updated since then (v1.2, v1.3 as of 2020–2021). The database is no longer actively maintained by WRI as of 2021–2022; GEM trackers have since become the preferred source for current plant status. Southeast Asian coverage, including Vietnam, is present but thinner than coverage for OECD countries — some Vietnamese plants are missing from the 2018 release. The technical note has no formal peer-reviewed journal publication and no minted DOI; citation must be to the WRI landing page and/or GitHub repository.
- **Role in citation mix.** Seminal. The canonical open global power plant database; widely cited in energy modelling literature as the primary reference dataset.

### Parzen et al. 2023 — PyPSA-Earth
*(new key: Parzen-Maximilian2023:pypsa-earth)*

- **Reference.** Parzen, M., Abdel-Khalek, H., Fedorova, E., Mahmood, M., Frysztacki, M. M., Hampp, J., Franken, L., Schumm, L., Neumann, F., Poli, D., Kiprakis, A., and Fioriti, D. (2023). PyPSA-Earth. A New Global Open Energy System Optimization Model Demonstrated in Africa. *Applied Energy*, 341, 121096. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2023.121096. arXiv: 2209.04663.
- **What the work did.** Presents PyPSA-Earth, the first open-source global energy system optimization model with full spatial and temporal resolution for any country in the world. Builds on the PyPSA framework by automating data retrieval from OpenStreetMap (grid topology), demand datasets, renewable resource data (Copernicus ERA5), and conventional generator data obtained via powerplantmatching (which in turn draws on WRI GPPD and GEM). Demonstrates the model on a Nigerian net-zero 2060 case study with 2-hourly resolution. The model's workflow is fully scripted via Snakemake, enabling reproducible runs for any country or region.
- **Why this paragraph cites it.** PyPSA-Earth is the direct upstream of our project context: the research group developing our benchmark works on PyPSA-based energy models for Southeast Asia (including Vietnam), and PyPSA-Earth is the global framework for such models. The citation makes two points: (1) PyPSA-Earth shows that sophisticated open energy models can retrieve data from open sources for any country, but (2) the conventional generator data in PyPSA-Earth is consumed, not produced — it flows from hand-curated databases (WRI GPPD, GEM) via powerplantmatching, with no AI generation step. The pipeline automates retrieval, not curation.
- **Limitations or critiques.** PyPSA-Earth's powerplant data quality is bounded by the quality of its upstream databases (WRI GPPD and GEM), which in turn are bounded by their coverage and freshness limitations. For Vietnam and Southeast Asia specifically, the global databases are thinner than for Europe or North America, which motivates country-specific data compilation efforts like PyPSA-VN and our benchmark. The model focuses on energy system optimization; data production quality is not evaluated against a gold standard.
- **Role in citation mix.** Recent (2023, peer-reviewed, *Applied Energy*). Represents the state-of-the-art global open energy system modelling framework; establishes that even the most advanced open modelling pipelines rely on hand-curated plant databases.

### Schlott et al. 2020 — PyPSA-VN
*(existing key in refs.bib: VGASHFPT)*

- **Reference.** Schlott, M., Schyska, B., Viet, D. T., Phuong, V. V., Quan, D. M., Khanh, M. P., Hofmann, F., von Bremen, L., Heinemann, D., and Kies, A. (2020). PyPSA-VN: An open model of the Vietnamese electricity system. In *2020 5th International Conference on Green Technology and Sustainable Development (GTSD)*. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1109/GTSD50082.2020.9303096
- **What the work did.** Builds an open energy system model of Vietnam's electricity system using the PyPSA framework. Assembles a thermal power plant inventory for Vietnam — covering coal, gas, and oil plants — from Vietnamese government documents, the National Power Development Master Plan (PDMP), and published reports. Runs capacity expansion and dispatch optimisation scenarios for Vietnam to 2050. The Vietnamese thermal plant data were compiled manually by the research team from primary Vietnamese-language and English-language government and regulatory sources; there is no automated data production step.
- **Why this paragraph cites it.** PyPSA-VN is the closest related project to our benchmark. It addresses the same object — Vietnamese thermal power plants — and uses the same modelling framework (PyPSA). The distinction is fundamental and must be stated explicitly: PyPSA-VN builds on manually compiled plant data; our benchmark evaluates whether AI systems can produce that kind of data automatically, and measures recall against an expert-compiled gold reference. PyPSA-VN's plant list is a predecessor of the gold standard we use; our work asks whether the effort required to compile such a list can be reduced by AI assistance.
- **Limitations or critiques.** PyPSA-VN was published in 2020 and reflects the Vietnamese electricity system as modelled with data available at that time; the Vietnamese power sector has changed substantially since (significant new capacity additions through 2023). The thermal plant data in PyPSA-VN may not reflect post-2020 additions. The model is a conference paper, shorter than a full journal article, and does not document the data collection methodology in detail. The Vietnamese plant inventory was compiled for energy modelling, not as a standalone data contribution; it has not been independently validated against a complete government registry.
- **Role in citation mix.** Recent/domain-specific (2020, IEEE conference). Closest related project geographically and methodologically; must be cited explicitly to show that our benchmark is not a reimplementation of an existing approach but a new evaluation task on top of the same domain.


## Related but not cited — justified

### Kruitwagen et al. 2021 — satellite-ML solar panel detection
*(Sharpest alternative: AI-assisted asset detection from imagery)*

- **Why not cited.** Kruitwagen et al. (2021, *Nature*, DOI:10.1038/s41586-021-03957-7) use convolutional neural networks trained on satellite imagery to detect and map 68,661 solar photovoltaic installations globally. This is a genuine AI-assisted power infrastructure data production approach — the kind of work most proximate to our claim that AI generation of energy databases is novel. However, it addresses renewable generation (visual objects in satellite images), not thermal plant attributes (fuel type, commissioning year, ownership) from heterogeneous document sources. The detection task — is there a solar panel in this pixel? — is fundamentally different from the tabulation task — does this document mention a plant, and what are its attributes? Our benchmark is about attribute extraction and recall from text, not spatial object detection. A referee raising this paper would receive this distinction.
- **Author note (2026-04-21).** Remote sensing is acknowledged as important and a future direction (v3: satellite + text fusion). The §7 Discussion should frame it as complementary, not as prior art we missed.

### Open Power System Data (OPSD)
*(The other major European open database; superseded by GEM and WRI GPPD for global scope)*

- **Why not cited.** The Open Power System Data project (Wiese et al. 2019, *Applied Energy*, DOI:10.1016/j.apenergy.2019.02.012) assembled open European power system data including conventional generator inventories, feed-in tariffs, and load time series. OPSD is one of the five databases merged by powerplantmatching. However, OPSD is European-only; our benchmark focuses on Vietnam, which is not in OPSD's scope. Citing powerplantmatching (which subsumes OPSD as an input) is sufficient and avoids listing every upstream data source. OPSD would be mentioned if the paragraph extended to the European database landscape; the paragraph focuses on resources relevant to our benchmark's geographic scope.

### Platts WEPP (S&P Global World Electric Power Plants)
*(Proprietary database; common reference in utility-sector work)*

- **Why not cited.** The Platts WEPP database is the most comprehensive proprietary global power plant database, covering 1.5+ million generation units with detailed technical attributes. It is commonly used as a benchmark in utility-sector analysis and is referenced in the powerplantmatching paper as a quality check. We do not cite it because (1) it is proprietary and inaccessible for open benchmarking, (2) citing a proprietary database as a reference in an open-benchmark paper would raise reproducibility concerns for referees, and (3) the open databases (WRI GPPD, GEM) are the ones we actually use and that PyPSA-Earth relies on. The contrast we draw — open hand-curated databases vs. AI generation — is cleanest with openly verifiable sources.

### EIA Form 860 (US Energy Information Administration generator inventory)
*(US-national registry; high quality but geographically limited)*

- **Why not cited.** The EIA Form 860 is the authoritative annual survey of US electric generators, providing plant-level capacity, fuel type, location, and commissioning year for all US generators above 1 MW. It is an excellent example of a government-maintained, expert-verified plant registry and illustrates the "hand-curated by expert teams" paradigm perfectly. However, it covers only the United States; our benchmark covers Vietnam. Citing a US-only database in a paragraph about energy data methodology would be misleading about geographic scope. EIA-860 would be appropriate in a methods section discussing gold-reference quality benchmarks; in §2, it is off-topic.

### Hannun et al. 2019 — ML for energy forecasting; broader ML-for-energy literature
*(ML applied to energy operations, not to database production)*

- **Why not cited.** A large literature applies machine learning to energy operations: load forecasting, renewable energy output prediction, fault detection. This work (representative: Hannun et al. 2019, *Nature Medicine*, applied deep learning to cardiac monitoring; more directly: various 2020–2024 ML-for-energy surveys in *Applied Energy* and *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*) does not address power plant database production. Applying ML to energy data is not the same as using AI to compile plant inventories. Including any paper from this tradition would blur the paragraph's focus on database production vs. energy operations modelling.
